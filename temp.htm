Plot of patient symptoms by weeks after treatment

In October 2026 McCairn posted this plot, where I added the annotations in red:

What are the horizontal error bars?

The x-axis seems to have a discrete scale and not a continuous scale, because the distance between week 1 and week 2 is the same as the distance between week 16 and week 24, so the interval between two ticks on the axis does not always correspond to a single week, but sometimes the interval is 8 or 12 weeks.

So the x-axis seems to have no units, but the x-axis just shows each week tiled horizontally next to each other. But then what are the units of the horizontal error bars?

The text at the bottom says: "horizontal scaling is a display convention, not treatment-time uncertainty". But what does that mean?

The "Horizontal half-width" is defined as half the SEM(tⱼ) divided by Wⱼ, where SEM is the standard error of the mean, t is "submission date (weeks)", j is "report group", and W is "nominal week label".

But if SEM(tⱼ) is a numerical value, then how is it possible to divide the numerical value with a "nominal week label"?

Grok said:

Division by a purely nominal label is not mathematically possible, and the formula shown in the figure therefore contains a clear notational problem. The expression for horizontal half-width is written as one-half times the SEM at time point j divided by W subscript j, yet the same figure later defines W as the nominal week label. A nominal label such as W1, W2, W3, W4, W16 or W24 is a categorical marker, not a number, so arithmetic division of a numerical standard error by that label is undefined.

For the formula to be executable at all, W subscript j must in fact be some numerical quantity associated with each report group rather than the label itself. The most coherent interpretation is that W subscript j stands for a weight (for example the number of patients who had reached that visit, a reporting-rate weight, or an effective sample size), while the phrase "nominal week label" refers only to the way the x-axis categories are displayed. Under that reading the horizontal scaling remains a pure display convention, exactly as the caption states, and does not represent uncertainty in treatment time.

An alternative possibility is that the authors simply inserted the numerical week values (1, 2, 3, 4, 16, 24) into the denominator while still calling the labels nominal. That would make the arithmetic possible, but it would also contradict the figure's own insistence that the labels are nominal and that the horizontal extent is not treatment-time uncertainty. Given that the graphic appears to be an informal update of the DFPA/SGF symptom data rather than a fully peer-reviewed methods paper, the inconsistency may simply be a presentational slip. In any event, if W subscript j is strictly the nominal label, the indicated division cannot be performed, and the precise numerical meaning of the horizontal half-width remains under-specified by the figure itself.

But I don't even understand what would be the purpose of the division by Wⱼ. If the horizontal error bars indicate the spread of sampling time in weeks (so for example some patients in the 24-week group got sampled 23.2 weeks or 24.5 weeks after treatment, and not exactly 24 weeks after treatment), and SEM(tⱼ) is the SEM of the time in weeks, then what is the point of dividing the SEM by Wⱼ? If the alternative possibility suggested by Grok above is correct, and Wⱼ for week 24 means the number 24, then was the SEM for week 24 divided by 24, and the SEM for week 2 divided by 2? That would explain why week 24 and week 16 have narrower error bars than earlier weeks, but I don't understand how it would make statistical sense.

I think the SEM also shouldn't have been divided by two, but rather one side of the error bar should be the width of the SEM, and both bars combined twice the width of the SEM. Grok said:

No, SEM divided by 2 is not a common measure of half-width in scientific figures or statistical reporting.

Standard error bars are almost always drawn so that the distance from the central point to the end of the bar equals the SEM itself (or SD, or half the width of a confidence interval). In other words, a conventional ±SEM error bar has a half-width of SEM, not SEM/2. The full vertical span of the bar is therefore 2 × SEM. Nothing in ordinary plotting conventions, error-bar definitions, or clinical-trial graphics treats half the SEM as the natural half-width.

The formula in the figure multiplies the entire fraction SEM(t_j)/W_j by 0.5. That factor of one-half is an arbitrary scaling choice made for visual display; it does not correspond to any established statistical quantity called a "half-width." Because the horizontal bars are already described in the caption as a display convention rather than a representation of uncertainty, the extra division by two simply further reduces their drawn length and has no independent interpretive meaning.
